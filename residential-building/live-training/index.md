### Open Book Test

The Virginia Residential Building Contractor examination is an **open-book test**. Candidates may bring permitted reference books, but the references are not provided at the testing site. PSI's Virginia contractor examination rules specifically allow reference books while prohibiting study guides.

The open-book format makes book familiarity an important part of preparation. Candidates should know which reference is most likely to contain the information required by a question and understand how to move efficiently through tables of contents, indexes, chapters, code sections, and technical material.

References may be highlighted, underlined, and indexed before the examination. Permanent tabs are permitted, while removable tabs are not. Reference materials containing handwritten notes are not allowed, and candidates may not write in the references during the exam session.

Live Virtual instruction can help candidates understand how different construction subjects connect to the references. During Self Study preparation, candidates can reinforce those skills by repeatedly locating information in the books and practicing how to identify the correct reference for different types of questions.

For example, a question asking for a specific residential code requirement may lead to the International Residential Code, while a practical construction question involving framing, carpentry, or building methods may be easier to research through Carpentry and Building Construction or the Builders Technical Supplement.

The goal is not to look up every answer. A strong open-book strategy combines construction knowledge with selective reference use. Candidates who understand the material and know their books can spend less time searching and more time working through the examination efficiently.

### Licensing Steps

Virginia contractor licensing is administered by the Virginia Board for Contractors. A contractor license consists of a license class and one or more classifications or specialties. The class determines the permitted level of contracting activity, while the classification or specialty determines the type of work the contractor may perform.

1. **Determine the appropriate contractor license class.** Virginia contractor licenses are issued as Class A, Class B, or Class C.
2. **Select Residential Building Contracting.** Residential Building is identified by the RBC classification abbreviation.
3. **Identify the Qualified Individual.** The contractor business must identify the individual responsible for qualifying the firm for the requested classification.
4. **Document applicable residential building experience.** Candidates seeking a classification that requires a technical examination must complete the applicable qualification and experience process.
5. **Obtain approval for the RBC examination.** Virginia identifies RBC as a classification requiring pre-approval and successful completion of the technical examination.
6. **Prepare for the examination.** Use Live Virtual instruction, Self Study preparation, practice-oriented review, and the applicable reference materials.
7. **Complete required contractor education and business requirements.** Requirements depend on the contractor class and the structure of the applicant business.
8. **Complete applicable business examinations.** Class A and Class B contractor licensing includes separate examination requirements for the Designated Employee.
9. **Submit the contractor license application.** The firm must provide the information and documentation required for its selected class and RBC classification.

The technical RBC examination is only one component of the overall contractor licensing process. Candidates should prepare for the exam while also organizing the business information and licensing documentation required for the contractor firm.

### Test Information and Study Materials

A productive RBC study plan can follow the natural progression of a residential construction project. Begin with layout and site work, then move into excavation and foundations. Continue through framing, exterior construction, insulation, ventilation, finishing, and related residential systems.

**Foundations and framing** deserve careful review because structural construction is central to residential building work. Candidates should understand foundation types, floor framing, wall framing, roof systems, structural openings, connections, and common residential building methods.

**Exterior construction** should include wall assemblies, weather protection, doors, windows, skylights, roofs, decks, walks, ramps, and exterior finish systems. Candidates should be prepared to connect practical building methods with applicable residential code provisions.

**Plans and specifications** are also important. Residential contractors work from drawings, details, dimensions, schedules, and written specifications. Candidates should practice interpreting construction documents and understanding how different parts of a plan set relate to the work being performed.

**Safety** should remain part of preparation throughout the course. Residential projects can involve excavation hazards, elevated work, ladders, scaffolds, power tools, framing operations, heavy materials, and changing jobsite conditions. Candidates should understand safe construction practices as part of competent contractor work.

If an answer is missed, locate the supporting material and review the surrounding section. This approach strengthens understanding instead of encouraging memorization of individual questions. It also teaches candidates where related information is located if a similar subject appears again.

Timed practice can help develop a realistic testing rhythm. Candidates should practice balancing what they know from construction experience with what they need to confirm through a reference. This helps make the open-book format a practical advantage rather than a reason to search unnecessarily.

## How does the Residential Building Contractor (RBC) exam work?

The Residential Building Contractor (RBC) exam is the technical portion of the license for that classification. A Class A or B firm usually also needs the business and law portion, and every applicant needs the 8-hour pre-license course.

- Virginia issues contractor licenses in three classes. Class C covers single jobs of $1,000 to under $10,000 and under $150,000 a year; Class B covers single jobs of $10,000 to under $120,000 and under $750,000 a year; Class A has no upper limit.
- Every applicant completes 8 hours of pre-license education before the license is issued.
- Class A and B firms name a designated employee or member of responsible management who passes the exams. PSI runs them, and the fee is $40 for one portion, $72 for two and $85 for three.
- The exams are open book. You bring your own references, which may be highlighted and permanently tabbed; removable flags and loose notes are not allowed.
