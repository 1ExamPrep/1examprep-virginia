### Exam Details

The **ICC Fire Plans Examiner F3 examination** contains **60 multiple-choice questions** and has a **3.5-hour time limit**.

The official examination content outline is divided into five major areas:

- **Administration – 5%**
- **Occupancy – 15%**
- **Hazardous Materials – 20%**
- **Fire Protection – 35%**
- **Egress Safety – 25%Fire Protection** is the largest examination category at 35%. Candidates should devote substantial preparation time to fire protection water supplies, automatic sprinkler systems, standpipes, alternative extinguishing systems, fire alarm and detection systems, portable fire extinguishers, and smoke control.

**Egress Safety** represents 25% of the examination. Candidates should be prepared to verify compliance of doors, stairs, ramps, balconies, exit access, emergency and standby power, emergency access to buildings, and conditions that may create hazards for firefighters.

**Hazardous Materials** represents 20%. This section includes hazardous materials management plans, safety data information, control areas, special hazards, flammable and combustible liquids and gases, and combustible storage.

**Occupancy** represents 15%. Candidates should be prepared to verify occupancy classification, construction type, occupant load, specific occupancy hazards, and special fire protection features.

**Administration** represents 5% and includes research and testimony, consultation and coordination, and recordkeeping associated with fire protection plan review.

### Open Book Test

The ICC F3 Fire Plans Examiner examination is an **open book test**. Candidates have three and a half hours to complete 60 multiple-choice questions and must follow current ICC rules governing allowable reference materials.

The testing period provides an average of approximately three and a half minutes per question. Although that provides time for code research, the examination covers several large technical references, so efficient navigation remains important.

A question involving occupancy classification, construction type, occupant loads, egress, or fire-resistance-rated construction may direct attention toward the **2021 International Building Code**. Questions involving hazardous materials, fire department access, fire protection systems, operational requirements, and fire prevention may point toward the **2021 International Fire Code**.

Questions involving sprinkler system design and installation should direct candidates toward **NFPA 13, 2019**, while fire alarm and signaling system questions may require **NFPA 72, 2019**.

Open-book preparation should include repeated practice identifying which reference applies before beginning a detailed lookup. Candidates should learn major chapter locations, tables, definitions, system terminology, and common cross-references.

### Test Information and Study Materials

A productive F3 study plan should follow the official examination weighting. Begin with **Fire Protection**, which represents 35% of the exam and is the largest content area.

**Water Supply for Fire Protection** represents 5% of the total exam. Candidates should understand fire flow, water supply adequacy, hydrants, fire department connections, hose connections, and related system-support requirements.

**Sprinklers, Standpipes, and Alternate Automatic Fire Extinguishing Systems** represent 12%, making this one of the largest individual job-task categories. Candidates should be able to identify when sprinkler or standpipe systems are required and review whether the proposed systems are designed to the appropriate standard.

Use **NFPA 13** to reinforce sprinkler design and installation principles. Review system types, sprinkler classification, spacing, positioning, obstructions, piping, water supply, and other major design considerations.

Plan review requires more than knowing that sprinklers are required. Candidates may need to determine whether layout, installation, operation, and monitoring information shown on the plans is consistent with the applicable standard.

**Fire Alarm and Detection Systems** represent 11% of the examination. Review when fire alarm and detection systems are required, how systems are classified, and whether equipment is listed and specified for the intended use.

Use **NFPA 72** to reinforce initiating devices, notification appliances, system design, monitoring, power supplies, emergency communications, and related signaling requirements.

**Smoke Control Systems** represent 5%. Candidates should be familiar with both mechanical and passive smoke-control concepts and understand when such systems are required and how they are documented for plan review.

Next, focus on **Egress Safety**, which represents 25% of the exam. Egress doors account for 6%, stairs, ramps, and balconies account for 6%, and exit access represents 7%.

Door questions may involve required number, size, swing, fire protection rating, panic hardware, or fire exit hardware. Candidates should read each question carefully because a single word describing occupancy, occupant load, or door function can affect the applicable requirement.

Stair, ramp, and balcony questions may involve width, rise and run, guards, rails, landings, and other dimensional conditions. Practice moving efficiently between the IBC egress provisions and related fire-code requirements.

Exit access questions can involve width, clearance, travel distance, illumination, signage, fire protection, and dead-end corridors. Candidates should understand how these factors work together as part of the complete means-of-egress system.

**Hazardous Materials** represent 20%. Study hazardous materials management plans and inventory statements, control areas, maximum allowable quantities, material classification, and special hazards.

Flammable and combustible liquids and gases account for 5% of the exam, while combustible storage accounts for another 5%. These are significant subtopics and should receive focused review in the International Fire Code.

Control-area questions require candidates to understand how hazardous materials are limited and separated within buildings. Study how quantities, construction, and location affect the use of control areas.

**Occupancy** represents 15%. Begin by reviewing occupancy classification and construction type. These two decisions influence many other fire and life-safety requirements.

Occupant load accounts for 4% of the exam. Candidates should be comfortable using occupant-load factors and verifying whether the specified occupant load for a room or area complies with code requirements.

Specific occupancy and use hazards account for 3%. Candidates should understand that different occupancies may require additional fire protection features because of their activities, contents, or risks.

Special fire protection features also represent 3%. Review fire-resistive materials, surface treatments, clearances from combustible conditions, and other special protective features identified on plans.

**Administration** represents 5%. Research and testimony, consultation, coordination, and recordkeeping are all part of the Fire Plans Examiner's professional responsibilities.

Fire Plans Examiners often coordinate with building officials, zoning officials, registered design professionals, owners, developers, contractors, and utilities. Candidates should understand the examiner's role in documenting findings and communicating technical requirements.

Reference navigation should be practiced throughout study. Learn to associate occupancy, construction type, occupant load, and egress with the IBC; hazardous materials and operational fire requirements with the IFC; sprinkler design with NFPA 13; and fire alarm system requirements with NFPA 72.

Practice under timed conditions. With 210 minutes for 60 questions, candidates have approximately three and a half minutes per question. Straightforward code questions should be handled efficiently so more time remains for detailed sprinkler, fire alarm, hazardous-material, or egress lookups.

When a practice question is missed, identify the reason. The error may result from selecting the wrong reference, misclassifying the occupancy, overlooking an exception, misunderstanding a sprinkler requirement, confusing a fire alarm provision, or applying the wrong hazardous-material limit.

A strong ICC F3 preparation routine combines building-code knowledge, fire-code knowledge, sprinkler-system familiarity, fire alarm knowledge, egress analysis, hazardous-material review, and repeated open-book navigation.

## How does the Fire Plans Examiner exam work?

The Fire Plans Examiner exam is a code official certification. It is taken after the Virginia Building Code Academy Core class and leads to DHCD certification.

- Virginia code officials are certified by the Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD), not by DPOR.
- The path is the Virginia Building Code Academy Core class, the classes for your certification, then the required exam, usually an ICC exam.
- Building, fire and property maintenance officials apply within 12 months of hire; technical assistants have 18 months.
- Certified officials complete 16 hours of DHCD-approved continuing education every 2 years.
