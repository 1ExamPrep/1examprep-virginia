Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Trade Examination for Electrical Contractors is part of the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. It is designed for Electrical Contractors, Master Electricians, and Unlimited Electricians and is accepted by participating state regulatory agencies, including the Virginia Board for Contractors.

NASCLA developed its electrical examinations through occupational analysis and input from electrical subject-matter experts. The examination program is intended to evaluate knowledge connected to the work electrical professionals perform in the field, including the ability to understand and apply electrical codes, standards, safety practices, and technical principles.

The examination content covers multiple areas of electrical contracting rather than focusing on a single book. Candidates should prepare for subjects involving electrical theory, Code requirements, wiring and protection, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, equipment, personal and jobsite safety, special electrical systems, project design and management, and other professional electrical topics.

Because the examination is based on practical electrical knowledge, preparation should combine technical understanding with reference navigation. Some questions can be approached through direct trade knowledge, while others may require a calculation or a lookup in the National Electrical Code, OSHA regulations, NFPA 70E, an electrical theory reference, or another permitted resource.

Virginia contractor licensing is administered separately from the NASCLA examination itself. Passing a NASCLA examination is not the same as receiving a Virginia contractor or master electrician license. Applicants must complete the applicable Virginia licensing process and satisfy the requirements established by the Virginia Board for Contractors.

State Requirements

The Virginia Board for Contractors, operating through the Department of Professional and Occupational Regulation, regulates contractor businesses and individual tradesmen working in electrical and other construction trades.

Virginia contractor firms are licensed as Class A, Class B, or Class C contractors. The contractor license applies to the business entity or sole proprietorship rather than functioning as an individual tradesman license. The business also carries classifications or specialties that determine the type of work it may perform.

Virginia requires contractor applicants for Class A, Class B, and Class C licenses to complete eight hours of Board-approved pre-license education. The pre-license course is a business-focused requirement addressing Virginia statutes, regulations, and contractor responsibilities. It is separate from electrical trade preparation.

For Class A and Class B contractor applicants, the required pre-license education must be completed by a member of responsible management or the designated employee. For Class C applicants, a member of responsible management must complete the course.

Virginia also requires every licensed contractor to identify a qualified individual for each specialty on the contractor license. For the Electrical Contractors specialty, Virginia regulations identify a Master Electrician tradesman license issued by the Board for Contractors as the required qualification for the qualified individual.

This distinction is important for electrical professionals. The Master Electrician license is an individual credential, while the Electrical Contractors specialty is attached to the licensed contracting business. A person can therefore encounter separate requirements related to individual master licensure and contractor-business licensing.

Virginia participates in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. NASCLA passing results may be transmitted to participating agencies through the NASCLA National Examination Database. The regulatory agency then pairs the examination result with the candidate's licensing application and evaluates whether the applicant meets its separate licensing requirements.

Passing the NASCLA Electrical Contractor examination does not create a national license and does not automatically issue a Virginia license. Applicants must still complete the Virginia licensing process, satisfy the state's application requirements, and meet the qualification standards associated with the credential they seek.

Test Information and Study Materials

The best way to use a highlighted and tabbed book package is to make the references part of your regular preparation routine. The tabs provide a practical way to move between major subjects, while highlighting helps important material stand out during study and review.

Begin with the 2023 National Electrical Code. Work through commonly encountered areas such as services, feeders, branch circuits, conductor sizing, ampacity, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, raceways, cables, boxes, motors, transformers, and equipment. Practice following cross-references and using tables instead of relying only on memorized article numbers.

Electrical theory should be studied through active calculations. Review voltage, current, resistance, power, circuit relationships, motors, transformers, and other electrical principles. For calculation problems, develop a repeatable process: identify known information, determine what must be solved, choose the appropriate formula, calculate carefully, and then determine whether a Code requirement affects the final answer.

Safety preparation should include both OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E. Learning how each reference is organized helps candidates distinguish construction-safety requirements from electrical workplace-safety requirements and locate relevant information more efficiently.

Project-management material should also remain part of the study plan. Electrical contractors work within projects involving planning, communication, scheduling, coordination, risk, documentation, and business responsibilities. These subjects complement technical electrical knowledge and can be important to professional contractor preparation.

Timed reference exercises can help candidates use the highlighted and tabbed format effectively. Select a practice question, identify the subject, navigate to the correct reference section, find the relevant information, and measure how long the process takes. Repeated practice can reveal which subjects need additional attention and which references require greater familiarity.

FAQ Section

Does Virginia participate in the NASCLA Electrical Examination Program?

Yes. The Virginia Board for Contractors is a participating agency in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program.

Is the NASCLA Master Electrical Contractor examination open book?

Yes. NASCLA's Accredited Electrical Examination Program includes open-book electrical trade examinations, including the Electrical Contractor examination.

Is a Virginia Master Electrician license the same as a Virginia electrical contractor license?

No. The Master Electrician license is an individual tradesman credential. A Virginia contractor license applies to the contracting business and includes the applicable license class and specialty.

What qualification is associated with Virginia's Electrical Contractors specialty?

Virginia regulations identify a Master Electrician tradesman license issued by the Board for Contractors as the qualification associated with the Electrical Contractors specialty.

Does Virginia require contractor pre-license education?

Yes. New applicants for Virginia Class A, Class B, and Class C contractor licenses must complete eight hours of Board-approved pre-license education according to the requirements for their contractor class.

Does passing the NASCLA examination automatically issue a Virginia license?

No. Passing the examination can satisfy an accepted trade-examination component, but candidates must still complete the Virginia licensing process and meet all applicable state requirements.

How should I use the highlighted and tabbed references while studying?

Use the books during regular study sessions rather than saving them for exam day. Practice Code lookups, calculations, safety searches, project-management review, and timed navigation so the tabs and highlighting become part of a familiar study process.

How does the NASCLA Electrical Contractor exam work?

The NASCLA Electrical Contractor exam is a tradesman exam: one person is licensed, and a contracting firm in that trade also needs its own Board for Contractors license.

  • Tradesman licenses are issued by the Board for Contractors through its Tradesmen program, for electrical, plumbing, HVAC and gas fitting.
  • The board has to approve you before PSI lets you schedule. The exam fee is $100 for journeyman and $125 for master.
  • Pre-license education is required, and renewals need 3 hours of continuing education for electrical, plumbing and HVAC and 1 hour for gas fitting.
  • Residential plumbing and residential HVAC mechanic licenses, added on April 1, 2025, need less experience than a journeyman license.
