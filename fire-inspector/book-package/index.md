The **ICC Fire Inspector II - 67** examination contains **50 multiple-choice questions** and has a **2-hour time limit**.

The official ICC examination outline divides the exam into four major content areas:

- **General Inspection Administration – 20%**

- **Occupancies – 22%**

**Occupancies** account for 22% of the examination. Candidates should be prepared to identify occupancy classifications, calculate allowable occupant loads, and conduct fire-safety inspections for different occupancy types.

**Regulated Materials and Processes** also represent 22%. This section includes industrial and commercial processes, storage and handling of regulated materials, and evaluation of interior finishes, furnishings, and decorative materials for code compliance.

**General Inspection Administration** represents 20% of the exam. This category covers communication, responding to inquiries, preparing inspection reports, research, permitting, recordkeeping, complaint investigations, appeals correspondence, legal proceedings, and scheduling inspection assignments.

The exam is designed to evaluate both code knowledge and the practical responsibilities of a Fire Inspector II. Candidates should be able to recognize a fire or life-safety issue, determine which code or standard applies, evaluate compliance, document deficiencies, and communicate findings appropriately.

### Open Book Test

The ICC Fire Inspector II - 67 examination is an **open book test**. Candidates have two hours to complete 50 multiple-choice questions and must follow current ICC examination rules regarding allowable reference materials.

The 2021 ICC Fire Inspector II study materials identify the **2021 International Building Code**, **2021 International Fire Code**, and **Fire Inspection and Code Enforcement IFSTA - 9th Edition** as the primary publications for preparation.

With 120 minutes available for 50 questions, candidates have an average of approximately two minutes and twenty-four seconds per question. Efficient reference navigation is therefore important. Candidates should learn to identify the subject of a question before beginning a detailed lookup.

A question involving construction type, occupancy classification, allowable building conditions, or certain means-of-egress provisions may direct attention toward the International Building Code. A question involving fire department access, fire-protection systems, operational requirements, permits, hazardous materials, or regulated processes may direct attention toward the International Fire Code.

Questions involving inspection procedures, reporting, communications, enforcement practices, or professional responsibilities may be supported by the IFSTA Fire Inspection and Code Enforcement reference.

Practice using all three books under timed conditions. Learn the tables of contents, indexes, chapter organization, defined terms, major subject areas, and commonly used tables. An open-book exam becomes much easier to manage when the references are familiar before test day.

### Test Information and Study Materials

A productive Fire Inspector II study plan should follow the official examination weighting. Begin with **General Provisions for Fire Safety**, which represents 36% of the exam. This is the largest single content area and includes several important inspection functions.

**Means of Egress** accounts for 6% of the overall examination. Candidates should review exits, exit access, exit discharge, doors, corridors, stairs, occupant movement, and other means-of-egress requirements relevant to inspected occupancies.

Fire Inspector II candidates should be able to evaluate whether an existing means-of-egress system remains usable and code compliant. Inspection questions may describe blocked exits, improper locking arrangements, inadequate clearances, or other conditions affecting safe occupant movement.

**Equipment and System Readiness** represents another 6%. Candidates should understand the purpose and condition of fire-protection equipment and systems, including fire alarm systems and other installed protection features.

Inspectors may need to determine whether equipment is operationally ready and whether identified conditions require correction. Practice recognizing the difference between inspecting a system's condition and evaluating detailed system design.

**Emergency Access** requires candidates to evaluate whether adequate emergency access is provided to a site. Fire department access can affect apparatus operations, firefighting, rescue, and emergency response.

**Fire Flow** is another part of General Provisions for Fire Safety. Candidates should understand the purpose of fire-flow testing and be able to evaluate fire-flow test information and associated data.

**Emergency Planning** requires candidates to evaluate emergency planning and preparedness procedures. This can involve understanding whether required procedures, plans, and occupant responsibilities are appropriate for the inspected property.

**Fire Protection** involves inspections of fire-protection features and systems. Candidates should be able to identify deficiencies and understand the relationship between installed systems, approved plans, and adopted codes.

The official outline also requires candidates to **compare approved plans with installed fire-protection systems**. This is an important advanced inspection responsibility because field installations should correspond with approved designs and code requirements.

**Occupancies** represent 22% of the examination. Begin by reviewing occupancy classification. Correct classification is important because fire and life-safety requirements often vary with the building's use.

Candidates should recognize common occupancy groups and understand how the use of a building or area determines classification. A change in use can also affect the fire-safety provisions applicable to the property.

**Occupant-load calculations** are a major part of the Occupancies section and represent 8% of the overall examination. Practice using occupant-load factors and determining the number of occupants permitted in a building or area based on its use and size.

Occupant load interacts with several other requirements, particularly means of egress. Candidates should understand how occupant count can affect required exits, egress capacity, and other life-safety provisions.

Inspection of different occupancy types represents 10% of the overall exam. Candidates should practice recognizing fire-safety concerns that may differ among assembly, business, educational, industrial, institutional, mercantile, residential, storage, and other occupancies.

**Regulated Materials and Processes** represent 22%. This section requires candidates to evaluate industrial and commercial operations, storage and handling of regulated materials, and interior finishes.

Industrial and commercial operations can involve processes that create unique fire or explosion hazards. Candidates should learn to identify the type of operation before navigating the applicable IFC provisions.

**Storage and Handling** is a significant subcategory representing 10% of the total examination. Candidates should be able to evaluate whether regulated materials are stored, handled, and used in accordance with applicable code requirements.

When studying hazardous or regulated materials, focus on terminology, classification, quantities, storage arrangements, protection features, and operational conditions. Knowing how the IFC organizes regulated-material provisions can greatly improve lookup speed.

**Interior Finishes** represent 4% of the exam. Candidates should understand how interior finish, furnishings, and decorative materials can affect fire growth and life safety and how their use is regulated by applicable code provisions.

**General Inspection Administration** represents 20% of the examination and should be studied as seriously as the technical code subjects. Fire inspectors regularly communicate with property owners, occupants, contractors, officials, and other stakeholders.

Communication questions may involve explaining fire prevention or fire-protection practices. Candidates should focus on clear, accurate, professional communication based on applicable code requirements.

Inspection reports are another important administrative responsibility. Reports should document observations and deficiencies in a way that supports enforcement, follow-up inspections, and recordkeeping.

**Research** requires inspectors to identify the applicable code or standard for a particular fire or life-safety issue. This reinforces the importance of knowing both the scope of the IBC and IFC and when additional standards may be relevant.

**Permitting** involves recognizing situations where permits are required and understanding the inspector's role within the permitting process.

Recordkeeping, complaint investigations, appeals correspondence, legal proceedings, and scheduling are also part of the official outline. These subjects reflect the administrative and enforcement responsibilities that accompany technical inspection work.

Use the **IFSTA Fire Inspection and Code Enforcement** book to reinforce these professional responsibilities. The IBC and IFC provide the technical code requirements, while IFSTA helps place inspection work into practical professional context.

Practice under timed conditions. With 120 minutes for 50 questions, candidates have approximately two minutes and twenty-four seconds per question. Familiar code questions should be answered efficiently so more time remains for calculations, regulated-material lookups, or complex inspection scenarios.

When a practice question is missed, identify the reason. The error may result from choosing the wrong code, confusing occupancy classifications, overlooking an exception, making an occupant-load calculation mistake, misunderstanding an administrative responsibility, or failing to recognize the regulated material involved.

A strong ICC Fire Inspector II preparation routine combines fire-code knowledge, building-code knowledge, practical inspection procedures, code-enforcement responsibilities, occupant-load calculations, regulated-material review, and repeated open-book navigation.

## How does the Fire Inspector II exam work?

The Fire Inspector II exam is a code official certification. It is taken after the Virginia Building Code Academy Core class and leads to DHCD certification.

- Virginia code officials are certified by the Department of Housing and Community Development (DHCD), not by DPOR.
- The path is the Virginia Building Code Academy Core class, the classes for your certification, then the required exam, usually an ICC exam.
- Building, fire and property maintenance officials apply within 12 months of hire; technical assistants have 18 months.
- Certified officials complete 16 hours of DHCD-approved continuing education every 2 years.
